Single nucleotide polymorphisms ([[concepts/single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)|SNPs]]) account for 83.6% of the total detected [[concepts/genetic variation|genetic variation]] in [[concepts/gene expression|gene expression]] levels across 14,925 transcripts in the International [[concepts/International HapMap project|HapMap]] population. This demonstrates that SNPs are the primary form of genetic variation influencing [[concepts/transcript|transcript]] abundance, substantially outweighing other sources of heritable variation. [@stranger_relative_2007]

## Synthesis
Research across multiple genomic studies establishes that single nucleotide polymorphisms dominate the genetic architecture of [[concepts/gene expression|gene expression]] variation, accounting for approximately 84% of detectable heritable differences in [[concepts/transcript|transcript]] levels across populations. This overwhelming contribution by SNPs occurs through mechanisms that are largely independent from those involving [[concepts/copy number|copy number]] variations, which capture a substantial but secondary fraction of approximately 18% of gene expression variation, with minimal overlap between the two signal types. The near-complete partition between SNP and CNV effects on gene expression suggests these variant types operate through distinct regulatory mechanisms, supporting the position that comprehensive phenotypic analysis requires interrogating both classes of [[concepts/genetic variation|genetic variation]] despite their unequal contributions. While the quantitative dominance of SNPs is firmly established through projects like the International HapMap, the precise mechanistic basis for why these variant types show such minimal overlap in their effects on gene expression remains an open question requiring further investigation.
