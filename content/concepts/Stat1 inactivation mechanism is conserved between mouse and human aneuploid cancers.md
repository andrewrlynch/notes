The mechanism by which [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploid]] [[concepts/tumors|tumors]] inactivate [[concepts/stat1 signaling|Stat1 signaling]] to suppress [[concepts/immune cell infiltration|immune infiltration]] is preserved and functional in human aneuploid cancers, indicating that this pathway represents a conserved evolutionary solution for chromosomally unstable cells to achieve malignant transformation. [@schubert_cancer_2021]

## Synthesis
The inactivation of [[concepts/stat1 signaling|Stat1 signaling]] in [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploid]] [[concepts/tumors|tumors]] represents a conserved evolutionary strategy across both mouse [[concepts/models|models]] and human cancers to evade the [[concepts/immune surveillance|immune surveillance]] that [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]] normally triggers. This mechanism is consistently coupled with increased Myc activity, forming a dual adaptation that allows chromosomally unstable [[concepts/tumor cells|tumor cells]] to suppress the [[concepts/immune cell infiltration|immune cell infiltration]] that would otherwise limit their proliferation and [[concepts/malignancy|malignancy]]. While the mechanistic relationship between Stat1 loss, [[concepts/myc activation|Myc activation]], and immune evasion in aneuploid cancers is well-established across species, what remains less clear is whether this represents the only viable pathway for [[concepts/aneuploid cells|aneuploid cells]] to overcome immune-mediated [[concepts/fitness|fitness]] costs, given that p53 loss—despite being common in many cancers—is notably not enriched in [[concepts/chromosomally unstable tumors|chromosomally unstable tumors]] specifically.
