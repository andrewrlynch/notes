## Synthesis
[[concepts/fitness|Fitness]], defined as the relative ability of an organism or variant to survive and reproduce in a given environment, is established across these sources as a quantifiable property that manifests at multiple biological scales and leaves measurable signatures in evolutionary history. At the cellular level, [[concepts/chromosomal imbalance|chromosomal imbalance]] through aneuploidy consistently impairs fitness regardless of which specific chromosome is affected, creating pleiotropic stress through proteotoxic burden, metabolic disruption, and reduced proliferation capacity across diverse experimental systems from [[concepts/haploid yeast|haploid yeast]] to mammalian cells. The mechanistic basis for fitness differences in rapidly evolving asexual populations operates through the gradual accumulation of [[concepts/small effect mutations|small-effect mutations]] along lineages, creating persistent [[concepts/fitness variation|fitness variation]] among [[concepts/circulating|circulating]] strains that becomes encoded in the branching topology of genealogical trees, thereby transforming phylogenetic analysis from a retrospective tool into a prospective method for predicting evolutionary success. A key unresolved tension emerges around fitness costs in [[concepts/cancer|cancer]] [[concepts/evolution|evolution]], where [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]] simultaneously imposes fitness penalties through enhanced [[concepts/immune cell infiltration|immune cell infiltration]] and [[concepts/immune surveillance|immune surveillance]], yet these costs can be circumvented through specific [[concepts/genetic alterations|genetic alterations]] such as combined [[concepts/stat1 inactivation|Stat1 inactivation]] and [[concepts/myc activation|Myc activation]], raising questions about the conditions under which [[concepts/aneuploid cells|aneuploid cells]] can evade the fitness burdens that appear universal in other experimental contexts.
