An increased rate of [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploidaneuploidy]] drives an elevated level of spontaneous [[concepts/lymphomas|lymphomas]] and lung tumors in aged animals. This demonstrates that elevated [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]] is associated with spontaneous tumor development, supporting Boveri's century-old hypothesis that aneuploidy causes [[concepts/tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]]. ## Synthesis
Studies establish that aneuploidy can promote [[concepts/spontaneous tumor formation|spontaneous tumor formation]] in aged animals, demonstrating an [[concepts/oncogenic|oncogenic]] capacity for [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]] in certain contexts. This [[concepts/tumor|tumor]]-promoting effect appears mechanistically linked to the ability of [[concepts/aneuploid cells|aneuploid cells]] to inactivate [[concepts/stat1 signaling|Stat1 signaling]] while simultaneously increasing Myc activity, thereby suppressing immune infiltration and enabling evasion of [[concepts/immune surveillance|immune surveillance]] despite the cellular stress imposed by chromosomal instability. However, this oncogenic role of aneuploidy exists in tension with findings showing that increased aneuploidy actually inhibits chemically and genetically induced [[concepts/tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]], revealing a context-dependent duality where the same genomic instability [[concepts/phenotype|phenotype]] can be either tumor-suppressive or tumor-promoting. The resolution of when aneuploidy acts as an oncogenic driver versus a [[concepts/tumor suppressor|tumor suppressor]] remains contested, though evidence suggests that spontaneous age-related tumor formation may represent a distinct biological context from experimentally induced carcinogenesis.
