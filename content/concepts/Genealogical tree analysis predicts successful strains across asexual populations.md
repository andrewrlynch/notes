A prediction method based on genealogical tree branching patterns can be applied to any [[concepts/asexual population|asexual population]] experiencing persistent [[concepts/selection pressure|selection pressure]] without requiring species-specific input. This approach successfully predicts successful strains, as demonstrated by predicting influenza progenitor lineages with near-optimal performance in 30 of cases. [@neher_predicting_2014]

## Synthesis
Genealogical tree analysis has emerged as a predictive tool for identifying successful strains in asexual populations by extracting [[concepts/fitness|fitness]] information encoded in branching patterns, with empirical validation showing informative predictions in 16 of 19 years of [[concepts/seasonal influenza|seasonal influenza]] data and near-optimal performance in 30% of cases when forecasting progenitor lineages. The mechanistic basis for this predictive capacity rests on the principle that tree topology reflects [[concepts/relative fitness|relative fitness]] differences among [[concepts/circulating|circulating]] lineages, which arise from persistent [[concepts/fitness variation|fitness variation]] maintained across influenza virus populations as [[concepts/small effect mutations|small-effect mutations]] accumulate over time. This approach transforms phylogenetics from retrospective description to prospective forecasting, though the synthesis reveals an implicit assumption that [[concepts/evolution|evolution]] proceeds through gradual accumulation of small-effect mutations rather than through large-effect changes or recombination events that could disrupt the fitness signals embedded in tree structure. The extent to which this predictive framework generalizes beyond influenza to other asexual populations and the conditions under which tree-based predictions fail remain areas requiring further investigation.
