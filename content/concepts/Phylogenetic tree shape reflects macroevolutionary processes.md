The shape of phylogenetic trees that connect [[concepts/extant taxa|extant taxa]] provides information about the [[concepts/macroevolutionary processes|macroevolutionary processes]] that generated them. [[concepts/tree shape|Tree shape]] can be analyzed to reveal patterns and dynamics of evolutionary change that would otherwise be obscured if only [[concepts/fossil record|fossil record]] data were considered. ## Synthesis
The structure of phylogenetic trees, defined by [[concepts/tree balance|tree balance]] (branching symmetry) and branch length distributions, serves as a quantitative record of the [[concepts/macroevolutionary processes|macroevolutionary processes]] that have shaped biodiversity over deep time. These shape characteristics mechanistically encode variation in speciation and [[concepts/extinction rates|extinction rates]] across evolutionary history, enabling researchers to detect signatures of major [[concepts/macroevolutionary phenomena|macroevolutionary phenomena]] such as [[concepts/mass extinctions|mass extinctions]] and [[concepts/adaptive radiations|adaptive radiations]]. [[concepts/tree shape|Tree shape]] analysis further allows investigators to test ecological and biogeographical hypotheses by correlating structural patterns with environmental changes and geographic events that may have driven rate shifts. While the relationships between tree shape features and macroevolutionary dynamics are well-established, the precise causal mechanisms linking specific ecological drivers to observed patterns in tree structure remain an active area of investigation.
