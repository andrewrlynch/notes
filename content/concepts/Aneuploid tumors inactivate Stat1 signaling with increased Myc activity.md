[[concepts/aneuploid|Aneuploid]] [[concepts/tumors|tumors]] specifically inactivate [[concepts/stat1 signaling|Stat1 signaling]] while simultaneously increasing [[concepts/myc activation|Myc activity]] to adapt to the stress induced by [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]]. This combined genetic alteration is enriched in [[concepts/chromosomally unstable tumors|chromosomally unstable tumors]] and represents a mechanism by which [[concepts/aneuploid cells|aneuploid cells]] achieve malignant transformation [[concepts/in vivo|in vivo]]. ## Synthesis
Multiple lines of evidence establish that [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploid]] [[concepts/tumors|tumors]] with [[concepts/chromosomal instability|chromosomal instability]] specifically inactivate [[concepts/stat1 signaling|Stat1 signaling]] while concurrently increasing Myc activity as a conserved mechanism to evade [[concepts/immune surveillance|immune surveillance]]. This dual genetic alteration directly counteracts the [[concepts/fitness|fitness]] cost that chromosomal instability normally imposes: [[concepts/chromosomally unstable tumors|chromosomally unstable tumors]] experience increased [[concepts/immune cell infiltration|immune cell infiltration]], but the combination of Stat1 loss and [[concepts/myc activation|Myc activation]] suppresses this [[concepts/immune response|immune response]] and allows [[concepts/aneuploid cells|aneuploid cells]] to persist despite [[concepts/aneuploidy induced stress|aneuploidy-induced stress]]. The mechanism is conserved between mouse and human aneuploid cancers, indicating an evolutionary solution distinct from the more common but non-enriched p53 loss pathway. While the immune evasion function of this dual alteration is well-supported, what remains contested is the broader question of whether aneuploidy acts primarily as [[concepts/tumor|tumor]]-promoting or tumor-suppressive, since increased aneuploidy both drives [[concepts/spontaneous tumor formation|spontaneous tumor formation]] in aged animals yet inhibits chemically and genetically induced [[concepts/tumorigenesis|tumorigenesis]] in other contexts.
