[[concepts/aneuploid|Aneuploid]] yeast strains that carry an extra copy of one or more chromosomes exhibit defects in [[concepts/cell cycle progression|cell cycle progression]]. This [[concepts/phenotype|phenotype]] was observed across multiple aneuploid strains regardless of which specific chromosome was duplicated, indicating that the cell cycle defect is a general consequence of [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploidaneuploidy]] rather than related to the identity of the extra chromosomes. ## Synthesis
Studies in [[concepts/haploid yeast|haploid yeast]] establish that aneuploidy consistently impairs [[concepts/cell cycle progression|cell cycle progression]] regardless of which specific chromosome is duplicated, revealing this effect as a general consequence of [[concepts/chromosomal imbalance|chromosomal imbalance]] rather than a gene-specific phenomenon. The mechanistic basis appears to center on proteotoxic stress: extra chromosomes produce additional proteins that create stoichiometric imbalances in cellular [[concepts/protein composition|protein composition]], leading not only to cell cycle delays but also to increased [[concepts/glucose uptake|glucose uptake]] and heightened [[concepts/sensitivity|sensitivity]] to protein synthesis interference. These shared phenotypic traits across different [[concepts/aneuploid|aneuploid]] strains—including proliferative disadvantages and metabolic alterations—indicate that the [[concepts/fitness|fitness]] costs arise from a universal proteostasis burden imposed by [[concepts/chromosome duplication|chromosome duplication]]. While the connection between protein imbalance and [[concepts/cell cycle defects|cell cycle defects]] is well-supported, the precise molecular checkpoints and pathways through which proteotoxic stress translates into delayed cell cycle progression remain areas for further investigation.
